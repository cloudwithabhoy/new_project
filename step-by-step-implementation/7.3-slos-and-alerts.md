# 7.3 — SLOs & alerts (error budgets · burn-rate rules · Alertmanager routing)

## Goal
After `7.1` and `7.2` you can *see* everything — metrics, logs, traces. But seeing isn't deciding. A wall of
green graphs at 3 a.m. doesn't tell you whether to wake someone up. In this part you set a concrete promise
for `checkout` (an **SLO** — e.g. 99.5% of checkouts succeed, p95 under 500ms), turn that promise into an
**error budget**, write **multi-window multi-burn-rate** alert rules that fire only when the budget is
genuinely at risk, and route those alerts through **Alertmanager** so the right severity reaches the right
channel with a runbook link attached.

## Why this part exists (read this first)
Phase `7.1` gave you metrics and `7.2` gave you logs and traces — raw signals. The classic mistake now is to
slap a naive threshold on them: "page me if error rate > 1%." That alert fires at 2 a.m. for a 30-second blip
nobody noticed, trains you to ignore it, and then stays quiet during a slow all-day degradation that actually
burns your users. Raw signals aren't decisions. You need to convert "the error rate is X" into "we are
spending our reliability promise faster than we can afford — act now."

The bridge from signal to decision is the **SLO + error budget** idea, and the smart alert on top of it is
**burn-rate alerting**. That's the whole subject of this part: define the promise, measure how fast you're
breaking it, and only page when the rate of breaking matters.

> NEW CONCEPT — SLI vs SLO vs error budget (the three words people mix up). Get these straight and the rest
> follows:
> - **SLI** (Service Level *Indicator*) — the actual measured number. "The fraction of checkout requests that
>   succeeded in the last 5 minutes = 99.93%." It's a ratio of good events to total events, computed from your
>   `7.1` metrics.
> - **SLO** (Service Level *Objective*) — the target you promise for that SLI. "Checkout availability ≥ 99.5%
>   over 30 days." A line in the sand.
> - **Error budget** — the *allowed* failure that the SLO implies. If you promise 99.5%, you're allowing
>   0.5% to fail. Over 30 days that 0.5% is a finite, spendable amount: your budget. Burn it slowly and
>   you're fine; burn it fast and you're heading for a missed SLO.
> The shift in mindset: 100% is the wrong target (impossibly expensive, and your users can't tell). The
> error budget is *permission to fail a little* — and the alert isn't "something broke," it's "you're
> spending that permission too fast."

## Step 1 — Write the SLI as PromQL and pick the SLO

> An SLI = a good/total ratio you can compute. An SLO = the number you promise that ratio will stay above.

First, define "good." For `checkout`, a request is **bad** if it returns 5xx (server failed it). So:

```promql
# the SLI: success ratio over the last 5 minutes (1.0 = perfect)
sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout", status!~"5.."}[5m]))
/
sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[5m]))
```
Read it: "rate of non-5xx checkout requests divided by rate of all checkout requests." That's your
availability SLI. (You'll define a latency SLI the same way — fraction of requests faster than 500ms — using
the histogram buckets from `7.1`.)

Now pick the **SLO**: **checkout availability ≥ 99.5% over 30 days**, and **p95 latency < 500ms**. 99.5% over
30 days means your error budget is **0.5%** of all requests — about **216 minutes** of "fully down" equivalent
per month. Write these numbers down; the alert math below uses them.

> Why 99.5% and not 99.99%? Each extra nine costs exponentially more (redundancy, on-call burden) and your
> users genuinely can't perceive the difference for a shop checkout. Pick the loosest SLO your users are
> happy with — that *maximizes* the error budget you get to spend on shipping features and absorbing blips.

---

## Step 2 — Burn-rate alerts (the smart part)

> NEW CONCEPT — burn rate, and why multi-window beats a simple threshold. **Burn rate** = how fast you're
> spending the error budget, measured as a multiple of "even" spending. Burn rate **1×** means you're using
> budget exactly fast enough to use up all of it in exactly 30 days — sustainable. Burn rate **14×** means
> you'll exhaust a whole month's budget in about two days — emergency.
>
> A naive alert ("error rate > 1%") has two failure modes: it screams at brief harmless spikes (noisy, you
> learn to ignore it), and it stays silent during a slow bleed that's quietly eating your whole month's
> budget. **Multi-window multi-burn-rate** fixes both by requiring two conditions at once:
> - a **fast/high** burn over a **short** window (e.g. 14× over 5 minutes) → page now, something's seriously
>   broken;
> - a **slow/low** burn over a **long** window (e.g. 3× over 1 hour or 6 hours) → ticket, you're degrading and
>   will miss SLO if it continues.
> Pairing a short and a long window with each rule is the trick: the short window makes the alert *fast* to
> fire and fast to *resolve*; the long window makes it *certain* the problem is real and not a 30-second blip.
> You alert on **how fast the budget is burning**, not on a raw error percentage.

### Step 2 — Express the error-budget burn rate in PromQL
The burn rate is just your error ratio divided by the budget. With a 99.5% SLO the budget is 0.005, so:
```promql
# error ratio over 5m, divided by the 0.5% budget = burn rate (×) over 5m
(
  sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout", status=~"5.."}[5m]))
  /
  sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[5m]))
) / 0.005
```
A result of `1` means burning at exactly budget pace; `14` means 14× — on track to blow the month in ~2 days.

### Step 2b — Write the alert rules as a PrometheusRule
> What's happening here: you create a **PrometheusRule** object. Just like ServiceMonitor told Prometheus
> what to *scrape*, PrometheusRule tells it what to *alert on* — the Operator loads it into Prometheus's rule
> evaluation for you.

Run `nano checkout-slo-rules.yaml`, paste:
```yaml
apiVersion: monitoring.coreos.com/v1
kind: PrometheusRule
metadata:
  name: checkout-slo
  namespace: monitoring
  labels: { release: kube-prom-stack }      # so the Operator picks it up (same convention as 7.1)
spec:
  groups:
  - name: checkout-availability-slo
    rules:
    # FAST burn: 14x over a short window → page immediately
    - alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetBurnFast
      expr: |
        (
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout",status=~"5.."}[5m]))
          / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[5m]))
        ) / 0.005 > 14
        and
        (
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout",status=~"5.."}[1h]))
          / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[1h]))
        ) / 0.005 > 14
      for: 2m
      labels: { severity: critical, service: checkout }
      annotations:
        summary: "checkout burning error budget at >14x (fast)"
        runbook_url: "https://runbooks.internal/checkout/error-budget"

    # SLOW burn: 3x over a long window → ticket, not a page
    - alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetBurnSlow
      expr: |
        (
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout",status=~"5.."}[6h]))
          / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[6h]))
        ) / 0.005 > 3
        and
        (
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout",status=~"5.."}[30m]))
          / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[30m]))
        ) / 0.005 > 3
      for: 15m
      labels: { severity: warning, service: checkout }
      annotations:
        summary: "checkout burning error budget at >3x (slow)"
        runbook_url: "https://runbooks.internal/checkout/error-budget"
```
Then apply it:
```bash
kubectl apply -f checkout-slo-rules.yaml
```
Read the fast rule back: "the 5-minute burn rate is above 14× **and** the 1-hour burn rate is also above 14×
— for at least 2 minutes — fire `critical`." That `and` across two windows is the multi-window trick: the
short window makes it react fast, the long window confirms it's not a blip. The slow rule uses 6h/30m at 3× —
a gentler, longer-lived condition that earns a `warning` ticket, not a 3 a.m. page. The `runbook_url`
annotation rides along to whoever gets paged.

What to expect: in the Prometheus UI under **Alerts**, both rules appear as `Inactive` (green) while
checkout is healthy. Force the fast one by breaking checkout briefly (scale it to 0, or point it at a dead
dependency) and watch it go `Pending` → `Firing`. Undo it and it clears on its own — that self-resolution is
the short window doing its job.

## Step 3 — Route alerts with Alertmanager (severity → channel)

> NEW CONCEPT — what Alertmanager does. Prometheus only *decides* an alert is firing; it doesn't know your
> Slack or PagerDuty. **Alertmanager** (already installed by the stack) takes firing alerts and **routes**
> them: it groups related alerts so you get one notification not fifty, silences ones you're already working,
> de-duplicates across replicas, and sends each to the right destination based on its labels. The label
> you set on each rule — `severity: critical` vs `severity: warning` — is exactly what it routes on.

### Step 3 — Configure routing and receivers
> What's happening here: you tell Alertmanager "critical → page the on-call (PagerDuty); warning → a Slack
> channel." You configure it via an `AlertmanagerConfig` object (the Operator-native way) or the chart's
> values; here's the config shape.

Run `nano alertmanager-config.yaml`, paste:
```yaml
apiVersion: monitoring.coreos.com/v1alpha1
kind: AlertmanagerConfig
metadata:
  name: ecom-routes
  namespace: monitoring
  labels: { release: kube-prom-stack }
spec:
  route:
    receiver: slack-warnings            # default destination
    groupBy: ["service", "alertname"]   # bundle alerts for the same service/alert into one notification
    groupWait: 30s                      # wait 30s to collect related alerts before first send
    groupInterval: 5m
    repeatInterval: 4h                  # re-notify every 4h if still firing
    routes:
    - matchers: [{ name: severity, value: critical }]
      receiver: pagerduty-oncall        # critical jumps the queue to the pager
      groupWait: 10s
  receivers:
  - name: slack-warnings
    slackConfigs:
    - apiURL: { name: alertmanager-slack, key: webhook-url }   # from a Secret
      channel: "#ecom-alerts"
      title: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.summary }}'
      text: 'runbook: {{ .CommonAnnotations.runbook_url }}'    # the runbook link lands in the message
  - name: pagerduty-oncall
    pagerdutyConfigs:
    - routingKey: { name: alertmanager-pagerduty, key: routing-key }
```
The Slack webhook and PagerDuty key are secrets — create them first, then apply the config:
```bash
kubectl -n monitoring create secret generic alertmanager-slack \
  --from-literal=webhook-url='https://hooks.slack.com/services/XXX/YYY/ZZZ'
kubectl -n monitoring create secret generic alertmanager-pagerduty \
  --from-literal=routing-key='<your-pagerduty-integration-key>'

kubectl apply -f alertmanager-config.yaml
```
Read the routing back: everything defaults to the Slack `#ecom-alerts` channel, but any alert labelled
`severity: critical` is matched by the sub-route and sent to PagerDuty instead — and faster (`groupWait:
10s`). `groupBy: [service, alertname]` means if checkout throws ten flavors of the same alert you get *one*
grouped notification, not ten pings. The `runbook_url` you set on the rule flows into the Slack message
`text`, so whoever responds has the fix-it doc one click away.

What to expect: trigger the fast rule again; within ~10s a message hits PagerDuty (or your test receiver).
Use Alertmanager's UI (`kubectl -n monitoring port-forward svc/alertmanager-operated 9093:9093` →
`http://localhost:9093`) to see the firing alert, which route it matched, and to add a **silence** while you
fix it.

## Concept deep-dive — SLOs and burn-rate alerting (turning signals into decisions)
**How it works.** Everything chains from one promise. The SLO ("99.5% over 30 days") defines an **error
budget** (0.5% of requests may fail). At any moment your SLI says how fast you're actually spending it; divide
that by the budget and you get a **burn rate**. You then alert on the burn rate over *two windows at once*:
```
   SLO 99.5%/30d ──► error budget = 0.5% ──► burn rate = (error ratio) / 0.005
                                                     │
              ┌──────────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┐
        short window (5m) ─ fast to fire & clear          long window (1h/6h) ─ confirms it's real
              └───────────────────── BOTH must exceed the multiplier ─────────────┘
                                                     │
                  14× ─► critical ─► PagerDuty           3× ─► warning ─► Slack
```
A page means "at this rate you will miss your monthly promise soon" — an actual decision to act — not merely
"a counter crossed a line."

**Why it fits.** Tying alerts to the budget aligns paging with what users feel: a brief blip barely dents the
budget, so it shouldn't page; a sustained 14× burn will blow the month, so it must. The two-window pairing
gives you both speed and certainty — the short window fires (and resolves) quickly, the long window vetoes
noise. And the severity label is the single knob that drives Alertmanager's routing, so "how bad" and "who
gets woken" stay in sync.

**Why not the alternatives.** A static threshold ("error rate > 1%") has no notion of budget: it can't tell a
harmless spike from a slow bleed, so it's simultaneously too noisy and too quiet. Alerting on raw symptoms
(CPU high, pod restarted) pages you for causes that may never affect users — the opposite of SLO discipline.
And alerting per-replica without Alertmanager's grouping/dedup buries the real signal under a storm of
duplicates. Multi-window multi-burn-rate on an error budget, routed by severity, is what makes your pager
*trustworthy* — every page is a real decision, so you stop ignoring it.

*Interview line:* "I alert on error-budget burn rate over multiple windows — a fast high-burn window to page
and a slow low-burn window to ticket — so paging tracks the SLO the user actually feels, and Alertmanager
routes by severity to the right channel with a runbook attached."
